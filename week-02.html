Day 1

Problem Identification & Japanese 5S Methodology

Observing Problems with Fresh Eyes

We started the week with Problem Identification, where we learned to observe friction points in physical and operational environments, analyze root causes, and formulate structured solutions.

We were introduced to the Japanese 5S Methodology — a world-standard operational framework for workplace efficiency, standardization, and discipline:

1. SEIRI
Sort
2. SEITON
Set in Order
3. SEISO
Shine
4. SEIKETSU
Standardize
5. SHITSUKE
Sustain

Hands-On Application: The Red Cubical

To put theory into direct practice, we applied the 5S method to organize and manage tools in the Red Cubical.

By sorting through necessary items, designating dedicated placements for every tool, cleaning the workspace, standardizing visual labels, and committing to sustaining order, we observed how spatial discipline significantly reduces retrieval time and enhances collaborative workflow.

Day 1 Takeaway: "Proper organization is not just about cleanliness; it is the operational foundation for efficiency, safety, and clear thinking."

Day 2

Algorithms & Think Like a Coder

The Podi Dosa Algorithm

On Day 2, we bridged daily life and computational logic by designing an algorithm for making Podi Dosa.

While it began as a familiar culinary task, writing down the exact algorithm forced us to deconstruct a multi-step routine into unambiguous, sequential instructions. We had to specify inputs (batter, ghee, podi), preconditions (hot tawa), exact procedural steps, and exit criteria (golden-crisp finish).

Heat Tawa → Pour Batter → Circular Spread → Drizzle Ghee → Sprinkle Podi → Flip & Toast → Serve Crisp

“Think Like a Coder” Masterclass

We completed all 11 episodes of the “Think Like a Coder” animated series.

The narrative challenges introduced key computational pillars:

Logic & Branching

Evaluating conditional decisions (if/else) and deterministic rules.

Loops & Iteration

Automating repetitive patterns efficiently without redundant code.

Decomposition

Breaking complex problems down into modular sub-tasks.

The series underscored that programming is fundamentally about structured thinking and algorithmic problem-solving before writing a single line of syntax.

Day 2 Takeaway: "Every complex human task is an algorithm in disguise. When you break it down into clear steps, anything can be solved."

Day 3

Rapid Creative Coding: Scratch 2-Minute Meme

Block-Based Programming Under Pressure

Day 3 was an energetic and creative session. We used MIT Scratch with a specific constraint: create an interactive meme within just 2 minutes.

The strict time pressure demanded rapid execution: sprite selection, costume transitions, audio soundbite syncs, and event-driven triggers.

Speed & Intuition

Rapid decision-making replaced hesitation, showing how visual block logic accelerates prototyping.

Creativity + Code

Humor and storytelling combined with broadcast messaging, delays, and loop mechanics.

This activity demonstrated how block programming demystifies code structure and proves that software development can be fast and highly engaging.

Day 3 Takeaway: "Strict constraints spark explosive creativity. Quick prototyping beats endless contemplation."

Day 4

App Development with MIT App Inventor: College Connect

Building “College Connect”

On Day 4, we built a fully functioning mobile application named College Connect using MIT App Inventor.

The app was conceived to solve a real campus information bottleneck: enabling students to effortlessly view and keep track of events, academic notices, inter-departmental updates, and student activities in one centralized hub.

Functional Highlights of College Connect

  • Centralized Event Feed: Aggregates notices and department activities cleanly.
  • Event-Driven Mobile Architecture: Button triggers, arrangement layouts, and multi-screen navigation.
  • Practical Impact: Shows how digital tools streamline student life and community coordination.

This hands-on session provided practical exposure to mobile UX design, block-based mobile logic, and live companion APK testing on smartphones.

Day 4 Takeaway: "The best applications are born out of empathy for everyday community challenges."

Laser Cutting & Vector Engraving

Lab Safety & Machine Specifications

Laser cutting requires rigorous safety protocols to manage high-energy thermal radiation, exhaust fumes, and chiller circulation. Before operating the machine, we verified the exhaust blower, cooling chiller, active air-assist, and confirmed only authorized materials were used (strictly prohibiting toxic PVC/chlorine plastics).

Parameter Specification / Value
Machine Make & Model FORGE 1490 CO₂ Laser Cutter
Bed / Working Area 1300 × 900 mm
Laser Tube Power 150 W CO₂ Tube
Control Software RDWorks (DXF vector exchange)
Material & Dimensions 2 mm Transparent Acrylic (50 × 50 mm blank)

Design Selection & Raster-to-DXF Vector Workflow

We selected the dynamic “FIRE IN THE HOLE!!” flame character graphic. This design was chosen specifically because it combines fine facial features, closed vector silhouettes, small text, and filled regions — testing both vector cutting/scoring and raster scan engraving.

1. Vector Conversion

Traced raster image into closed contours, eliminated duplicate lines and noise fragments.

2. Scaling & Margins

Scaled artwork to 45 × 45 mm, preserving safe boundary margins on the 50 mm acrylic blank.

3. RDWorks Layer Setup

Separated Laser Scan layer (engraving) and Laser Cut layer (boundary scoring) at 100 mm/s & 30% power.

Laser Parameters & Recorded Simulation

Speed 100 mm/s
Power (Min/Max) 30% / 30%
Simulated Time 1 min 14.3 s
Laser Light Time 28.38 s

Troubleshooting & Engineering Insights

During vector preparation, duplicate contours caused excessive localized heat on acrylic. By cleaning vector geometry in Illustrator and testing the toolpath in RDWorks simulation, the finished transparent acrylic sample achieved sharp, clean contrast without melting or scorching.

Laser Cutting Takeaway: "Digital fabrication quality is won in vector preparation. Clean paths and tuned power settings turn raw acrylic into precise physical artwork."
Digital Fabrication • Segment 2

3D Printing & Additive Manufacturing

Additive Manufacturing vs. Subtractive Machining

We explored the core principles of Fused Deposition Modeling (FDM). While subtractive machining (milling/CNC) removes material and struggles with deep undercuts and enclosed cavities, additive manufacturing constructs objects layer-by-layer directly from an STL surface mesh.

Machine Parameter Bambu Lab H2S Specification
Printer Architecture CoreXY High-Speed FDM
Build Volume 340 × 320 × 340 mm
Nozzle Setup 0.4 mm Hardened-Steel (Max 350 °C)
Heat Bed & Chamber PEI Textured Bed (120 °C) & Heated Chamber (65 °C)
Slicing Software Bambu Studio

Selected Model: Organic Toothless Dragon

We selected an organic Toothless-style dragon model (bounding envelope 45.7 × 98.7 × 16.2 mm) featuring curved wings, horns, tail segments, and subtle overhangs — ideal for evaluating layer resolution and support structures.

Calibrated Bambu Studio Slicer Settings

Layer Height 0.20 mm
Nozzle Temp 220 °C (PLA)
Bed Temp 55 °C
Infill Pattern 15% Gyroid
Wall Loops 2 Walls
Print Speed 200 mm/s
Model Material 8.50 g (2.81 m)
Total Print Time 37m 47s

The Gyroid infill ensured multi-directional mechanical strength while maintaining minimal weight. The automated supports consumed only 0.11 g of filament, ensuring effortless post-print cleanup and crisp surface finish without leaving scarring on the visible organic contours.

3D Printing Takeaway: "Additive manufacturing removes conventional geometric limitations. Slicing orientation and support economy make complex organic prototyping accessible."
