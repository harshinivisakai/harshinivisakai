Segment 01

Laser Cutting & Vector Engraving

1. Laboratory Safety & Strict Protocols

Operating high-energy CO₂ lasers requires strict adherence to thermal, electrical, and optical safety standards. Before initializing any job:

  • Exhaust Ventilation: The blower system was activated to continuously extract acrylic fumes and smoke particles.
  • Water Chiller Cooling: The CO₂ tube cooling circulation was verified to prevent thermal stress on the laser tube.
  • Active Air Assist: High-pressure lower blowing was kept engaged to suppress flames and blow debris away from the cutting focal point.
  • Material Verification: Used only laboratory-approved 2 mm transparent acrylic. Chlorine-based plastics (such as PVC) are strictly prohibited due to corrosive and toxic gas emissions.

3. Selected Artwork & Image-to-DXF Vector Workflow

We selected the dynamic “FIRE IN THE HOLE!!” flame character graphic. This design was specifically chosen because it blends delicate facial features, curved silhouettes, bold typography, and dense filled areas — serving as an ideal benchmark for testing both vector line cutting and raster scan engraving.

1. Vector Conversion

Converted raster artwork into clean, closed vector paths; stripped unwanted noise and overlapping trace artifacts.

2. Scaling & Margins

Scaled artwork to 45 × 45 mm, leaving a clean protective margin inside the 50 × 50 mm acrylic blank.

3. RDWorks Layer Setup

Assigned Laser Scan layer (for engraving details) and Laser Cut layer (for outer boundary scoring) at 100 mm/s & 30% power.

4. Result & Problem Solving

During preliminary conversion, duplicate vector contours caused localized over-burning. By manually pruning overlapping paths in Illustrator before exporting to DXF, we eliminated redundant laser passes and achieved clean, frosted white engraving on the transparent acrylic with crisp character clarity.

Laser Cutting Takeaway: "Digital fabrication quality is determined during vector preparation. Clean geometry, closed paths, and calibrated power settings produce crisp physical results."
Segment 02

3D Printing & Additive Manufacturing

1. Additive vs. Subtractive Manufacturing

While subtractive CNC machining removes material from a solid billet using rotational cutters (facing severe reach limitations with internal undercuts and organic geometries), Fused Deposition Modeling (FDM) constructs complex geometries layer by layer directly from an STL polygon surface mesh.

Machine Feature Bambu Lab H2S Specification
Kinematics & Platform CoreXY High-Speed Enclosed FDM
Build Volume 340 × 320 × 340 mm
Nozzle Configuration 0.4 mm Hardened-Steel (Max 350 °C)
Bed & Thermal Chamber Textured PEI Plate (120 °C) & Active Chamber Heating (65 °C)
Slicing Environment Bambu Studio (with automatic tree support calculation)

2. Selected STL Model: Organic Toothless Dragon

We selected an intricate Toothless dragon model (bounding envelope 45.7 × 98.7 × 16.2 mm) featuring sweeping wings, horns, curled tail segments, and subtle overhangs. This model served as a rigorous test for layer stacking, overhang angle thresholds, and support economy.

3. Quantitative Material & Execution Results

The slicing strategy utilized Gyroid infill for isotropic mechanical integrity without adding excess weight. Automatic supports accounted for only 0.11 g ($0.04\text{ m}$) of the total $8.62\text{ g}$ extrusion. This minimal support ratio allowed effortless post-processing without surface scarring, while capturing all subtle curvatures on the dragon's head and wings.

3D Printing Takeaway: "Additive manufacturing removes conventional geometric constraints. Smart orientation and minimal support usage make complex organic prototyping fast and sustainable."
